Dental crowns in West Alexandria

A crown is a cap that covers the whole visible part of a tooth. It is what we reach for when a tooth has lost too much of itself to a crack, a large old filling or a root canal, and a filling would only be patching a wall that is about to give way.

Why a filling is sometimes the wrong repair

A filling replaces the part of a tooth that was lost. It relies on the tooth around it being strong enough to hold it. When more than about half of the tooth is filling, or when a crack runs through the remaining walls, every bite loads thin enamel that was never meant to carry the work alone. Teeth like that tend to fail suddenly, often on something soft.

A crown turns the problem around. Instead of the tooth holding the repair, the repair holds the tooth. It wraps the whole chewing surface and the sides, so biting force is spread over the crown and down the root instead of prying the walls apart.

That is also why a back tooth almost always gets a crown after root canal therapy. The tooth is hollowed out and no longer has a live nerve warning you when you bite too hard. A crown is what keeps it from splitting later.

Signs a tooth may need a crown

  • A large filling that has chipped, cracked or keeps breaking at the edges.
  • A crack you can feel with your tongue, or a sharp pain when you bite and release.
  • A tooth that has had a root canal and still has only a filling on top.
  • A tooth worn flat or short from years of grinding.
  • A tooth that is badly discolored or misshapen and a veneer would be too thin to hold.
  • A single tooth replaced with an implant, which needs a crown as its final piece.

How it goes, visit by visit

Most crowns take two appointments, about two weeks apart.

  1. The decision

    We look at the tooth and the X-ray together on the screen. If a crack has reached the nerve or the root, that changes the plan, and we tell you before anything starts. You get the cost in writing.

  2. Preparation

    The tooth is numbed and shaped so the crown has room to sit without being bulky. Any decay or old filling is removed and a core is built up if a lot of tooth is missing. This is the longest part, usually under an hour.

  3. The impression

    A record of the prepared tooth and the teeth it meets goes to the dental laboratory that makes the crown, along with a shade matched to your other teeth.

  4. The temporary

    You leave with a temporary crown cemented in place. It protects the tooth and keeps the space. Chew on the other side where you can and avoid sticky foods.

  5. Fitting

    At the second visit the temporary comes off, the new crown is tried in, and the bite and the contacts with the neighboring teeth are checked and adjusted until it feels like your own tooth. Then it is cemented for good.

What a crown is made of

Most crowns here are all-ceramic. Modern ceramics are strong enough for back teeth and can be matched closely to the color and translucency of the teeth beside them, so a crown on a front tooth does not announce itself.

For a very heavy bite, or a molar that takes a lot of grinding, we may suggest a stronger ceramic or a metal-based crown. Which one makes sense depends on where the tooth is, how hard you bite and what you want it to look like, and we will explain the trade-offs rather than deciding for you.

If you grind at night, a night guard protects the crown the same way it protects your natural teeth. Ceramic is hard, but a nightly grinding habit wears down anything.

What it feels like afterward

The numbness wears off over two or three hours. The gum around the tooth may be tender for a day or two from the work, and the tooth can be sensitive to cold for a little while, especially if the preparation was deep. That settles.

A bite that feels high is the one thing not to wait on. If the crown touches first when you close, call and we adjust it in a few minutes. Left alone, a high crown makes the tooth sore and can crack the ceramic.

With daily brushing and flossing, and check-ups so we can watch the edge where crown meets tooth, a crown will usually last well over a decade. The crown itself cannot decay, but the tooth underneath can if plaque sits at the margin.

Lost a crown or broken one?

Keep the crown, keep the tooth clean, and call. A crown that has come off whole can often be re-cemented, and the sooner it goes back the better the tooth underneath does. Our hours run until 8 PM on weekdays and we are open weekend mornings.

Straight answers

Does getting a crown hurt?

The tooth is fully numbed before any shaping starts, and most people say the preparation feels like a long filling appointment. The gum can be tender for a day or two afterward. If you are anxious about the visit, say so when you book and we can plan for nitrous oxide or other comfort options.

How long does a crown last?

Ten to fifteen years is typical, and many last longer. What shortens a crown's life is decay at the edge where it meets the tooth, grinding without a night guard, and a bite that was never adjusted. All three are things check-ups catch early.

Can a crown be done in one visit?

Our crowns are made by a dental laboratory, which is why there are two visits with a temporary in between. The lab-made crown is fitted and checked at the second visit before it is cemented for good.

Will the crown look like my other teeth?

Yes. The shade is matched to the teeth beside it before the crown is made, and all-ceramic crowns have the same slight translucency as enamel. On a front tooth we take extra care with the shape and the edge at the gum line.

Does insurance cover crowns?

Most dental plans cover a share of a crown when it is needed to restore a tooth, and the amount varies by plan and annual maximum. We check your benefits and give you a written estimate before the first visit so there are no surprises. Not sure what your plan does? See understanding dental insurance.

What if the tooth is too broken for a crown?

Sometimes a tooth is cracked below the gum or has too little left to hold a crown. We will tell you honestly if that is the case and go through the options, which usually means replacing the tooth with an implant or a bridge.
