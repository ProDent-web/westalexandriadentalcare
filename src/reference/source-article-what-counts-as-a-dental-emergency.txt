[h1] What Counts as a Dental Emergency?
A calm, practical guide to knowing when to call a dentist, when to seek hospital care and what you can safely do in the meantime.
August 21, 20267 min readReviewed for patient education
Dental problems rarely appear at a convenient time. A tooth may begin throbbing during dinner, a crown may loosen before work, or a child may fall and injure a front tooth on the weekend. The difficult part is often deciding how urgently to respond.
A dental emergency generally includes significant pain, infection, uncontrolled bleeding or an injury that could threaten a tooth or the surrounding tissues. Other problems may not be true emergencies, but they still deserve a prompt call. A dental team can assess the timing and help you choose the safest next step.
[h2] Signs you should call a dentist promptly
Contact a dentist as soon as possible if you experience:
Severe, persistent or worsening tooth pain
Swelling of the gums, face or jaw
A painful bump, drainage or bad taste that may signal infection
A broken, cracked, displaced or knocked-out tooth
Bleeding from the mouth that is difficult to control
Trauma to the teeth, gums or jaw
A lost filling or crown that causes pain or leaves a sharp edge
Significant pain or swelling after a dental procedure
Difficulty opening your mouth with pain or swelling
Symptoms alone do not reveal the cause. Similar pain can come from decay, a cracked tooth, gum inflammation, infection or another condition. A clinical examination—and sometimes dental imaging—is needed before a diagnosis or treatment recommendation can be made.
[h2] When to call 911 or go to an emergency room
Some situations extend beyond the dental office. Call 911 or seek emergency medical care for trouble breathing or swallowing, rapidly increasing facial or neck swelling, severe facial trauma, loss of consciousness, or bleeding that remains uncontrolled.
A hospital can address immediate threats to breathing, circulation and serious injury. Dental treatment may still be needed afterward to address the tooth or source of infection. If symptoms appear severe or are progressing quickly, choose the safer level of care.
[h2] What to do after a tooth is knocked out
A knocked-out permanent tooth is time-sensitive. Pick it up by the crown—the part normally visible in the mouth—not by the root. If it is dirty, rinse it gently without scrubbing or removing attached tissue.
If possible, place the permanent tooth back in its socket without forcing it. If that is not practical, keep it moist in milk or an ADA-accepted tooth-preservation product and seek dental care immediately. Do not allow the tooth to dry out.
Do not attempt to place a knocked-out baby tooth back in its socket. Doing so may damage the developing permanent tooth. Call a dentist promptly for guidance and evaluation.
[h2] What you can do for pain, swelling or a broken tooth
For a toothache, rinse gently with warm water and use floss carefully if food may be trapped between the teeth. Do not place aspirin directly against the tooth or gums because it can injure soft tissue.
A cold compress against the outside of the face may help after an injury or with localized swelling. If a tooth breaks, collect any pieces you can find and bring them to the visit. Avoid biting on the affected side.
Over-the-counter pain medicine may be reasonable for some patients when taken exactly as labeled, but it is not safe for everyone. Consider your medical conditions, allergies, pregnancy status and other medications, and ask a healthcare professional if you are unsure. Pain relief does not treat the underlying cause.
[h2] What happens at an emergency dental visit?
The first goal is to understand the problem and reduce immediate risk. Your visit may include a review of symptoms and medical history, an examination and appropriate imaging. Depending on the findings, care might involve stabilizing a tooth, addressing infection, repairing damaged tooth structure or planning follow-up treatment.
An emergency visit does not automatically mean an extraction or a major procedure. The correct recommendation depends on the tooth, surrounding tissues, extent of damage and your overall health. [h2] Quick dental emergency FAQ
[h3] Is every toothache an emergency?
Not necessarily, but severe, persistent or worsening pain should be evaluated promptly. Call even if you are unsure.
[h3] Can antibiotics fix a dental infection?
Antibiotics may be appropriate in certain cases, but they do not replace treatment of the source. A dentist must evaluate the situation.
[h3] Should I go to the ER for facial swelling?
Seek emergency medical care if swelling affects breathing or swallowing, spreads rapidly, involves the neck or accompanies severe systemic symptoms.
