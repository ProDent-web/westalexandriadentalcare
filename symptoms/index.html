Symptom guides

Start with what you feel.

You do not need to know the dental term. These guides begin with the thing you noticed, a zing from cold water, gums that bleed when you floss, a jaw that clicks, and explain what is usually behind it, what you can do at home tonight, and how soon it should be looked at.

How to use these guides

  1. Find the symptom

    Each guide covers one thing you might notice. If two apply, read both; the causes often overlap.

  2. Know how soon to act

    Each guide says how quickly the problem should be looked at: within a few weeks, within days, or today. That is the first thing to read.

  3. Do the home steps

    Simple things that help until the visit: how to rinse, what to avoid, which pain relief is sensible, what to notice and tell us.

  4. Book or call

    Most symptoms want an appointment within a week or two. Pain, swelling or an injury want a call today. Booking is online around the clock, and we are open seven days.

Symptoms that usually point to the gums

  • Bleeding when you brush or floss, which is inflammation rather than injury. See bleeding gums.
  • Gums that have pulled back and teeth that look longer. See gum recession.
  • Puffy, tender or swollen gums, in one spot or everywhere. See swollen gums.
  • A tooth that moves when you press it. See loose tooth.
  • Breath that does not improve with brushing. See bad breath.

Symptoms that usually point to a tooth or the jaw

  • A sharp zing from cold, sweet or air. See tooth sensitivity.
  • Pain when you bite and let go. See cracked tooth.
  • A tooth that still hurts weeks after a filling. See tooth pain after a filling.
  • A sore, clicking or locking jaw, or morning headaches. See jaw pain and teeth grinding.
  • Pain at the very back of the mouth in your late teens or twenties. See wisdom tooth pain.
  • A dry, sticky mouth, or snoring and unrefreshing sleep. See dry mouth and snoring.

A note on reading symptoms online

A symptom narrows the possibilities; it does not make the diagnosis. The same cold sensitivity can come from a worn spot, a cavity, a crack or a new filling settling, and the fix is different for each. These guides are written to help you decide how urgently to act and what to expect, not to replace an exam. When you are in the chair, the answer is usually quick: an air test, a look, sometimes an X-ray shown on the screen beside you.
